### 1.4 Sinha & Kapur (2021), *When problem solving followed by instruction works* [FT — new]
*Review of Educational Research* 91(5), 761–798. Open access: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.3102/00346543211019105 ; PDF: https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf

PS-I means problem solving first, then instruction. I-PS means instruction first, then problem solving.

**Overall results**
- 53 studies, 166 comparisons.
- PS-I vs I-PS: g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51] for conceptual knowledge and transfer.
- Procedural knowledge: g = −0.03, not significant.
- Adjusted for publication bias: g = 0.87.

**Age (Table 4)**
- Grades 2–5: g = **−0.09**. The CI is printed as [−0.92, −0.16], which does not bracket the estimate; that is probably a typesetting error.
- Grades 6–10: g = 0.50.
- Undergraduates: g = 0.28.
- Grades 2–5 made up only **25 of 166 comparisons (15.1%)**.
- The authors call the age differences "(marginally) significant". The grades 2–5 average "favoring I-PS was low".

**Domain (Table 5)**
- Domain-general skills: g = **−0.17** [−1.11, −0.02], favouring instruction first.
- The authors' explanation: in such tasks "the problem-solving phase in and of itself is less likely to provide implicit feedback about what goals to adopt".

**Time span**
- A few hours g = 0.32 vs a few days g = 0.41. Not significant.

**New, design-relevant findings**
- *"Cramming too many design features within a short amount of time may not be optimal"*: in interventions lasting a few hours, higher fidelity to the full Productive Failure design was associated with *lower* effects (β = −0.0116, p = .055).
- *"For shorter interventions, affective draw of the problem may be critical"* (β = 0.73, p = .07).
- For younger pupils, the authors suggest "scaffolding the initial problem-solving phase … might be critical". Possible reasons they give: prior knowledge that is activated but not relevant, and weak metacognitive strategies such as "self-explaining, debugging, and error-evaluation".
- They note that "educational games comprising storylines with teachable moments tightly integrated into the gameplay experience are not naturally described as either I-PS (or PS-I)".

**What this means for DeltaOps**
- The reversal for grades 2–5 is real but small, and it rests on few comparisons.
- The stronger signals are these. For 7–10-year-olds and for a domain-general skill such as "give clear instructions":
  - scaffold the failure, so it is not left open-ended
  - make the goal explicit
  - follow the failure quickly with a clear explanation
  - keep each short session to a few strong design moves rather than many.

### 1.6 Habgood & Ainsworth (2011), *Motivating children to learn effectively* [FT — new]
*J. Learning Sciences* 20(2), 169–206. Record: https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/ ; accepted manuscript: https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf

**Design (Study 1)**
- N = 58 children in a UK primary school (30 girls, 28 boys).
- Ages 7 years 1 month to 8 years 10 months (mean 8 years 0 months), taught under the Key Stage 2 Numeracy Strategy.
- Three versions of the maths game *Zombie Division*:
  - intrinsic: the division happens through combat
  - extrinsic: a quiz at the end of each level
  - control: no maths.
- **Dose:** sessions of 15–20 minutes, 135 minutes of play in total, 4 hours in the study, spread over 34 days.

**Debrief**
- The authors call post-play debriefing "critical". After about 40 minutes of play, the same teacher gave each group a **30-minute reflection lesson**: 15 minutes of direct instruction, then 10 minutes of paired exercises.

**Results**
- All groups improved: the mean rose from 31% at pre-test to 46% at delayed test.
- Group differences appeared **only at the delayed test** (2 weeks after the post-test): intrinsic 58%, extrinsic 41%, control 38%; F(2,48) = 7.49, partial η² = 0.24.
- The manuscript prints the intrinsic-vs-extrinsic post hoc as "p < 0.14" while calling it significant. This may be a typo for .014; the paper does not resolve it.
- **The control group also improved significantly.** The authors write: "This illustrates the power of debriefing in combination with the motivational appeal of games."
- During play, the extrinsic group was *more* accurate but did fewer maths tasks.

**Study 2**
- N = 16, ages 9 years 10 months to 11 years 2 months, after-school club, free choice of version.
- Children played the intrinsic version for 75.7 minutes vs 10.28 minutes for the extrinsic one (t(15) = 7.38, p < .001), "over seven times longer".

**Caveats and implications for DeltaOps**
- Small samples, one game, one domain (maths).
- Effects emerged after a delay and alongside a teacher debrief.
- The paper supports two DeltaOps principles: integrate the lesson into the core mechanic, *and* always debrief. It does not support claims about single sessions.

### 1.13 Druga, Williams, Breazeal & Resnick (2017), *"Hey Google is it OK if I eat you?"* [FT — new]
IDC '17. https://robots.media.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2017/06/idcwp0180-drugaACR.pdf

**Design**
- N = 26: 12 children aged 3–4 and 14 aged 6–10. Four did not finish.
- Children met four agents (Alexa, Google Home, Cozmo, Julie chatbot) at 15-minute stations.
- After each station they completed a 10-item sticker "monster game" questionnaire.

**Findings**
- "Overall, most participants agreed that the agents are friendly … and trustworthy."
- "The younger children (3-4 years old) experienced difficulty interacting with the conversational and chat agents."
- On whether the agent was smarter than the child: 100% of older children said Alexa was smarter. Ratings for the other agents varied, but older children never said Alexa or Google Home was less smart than them.
- Older children "related the agent's intelligence to its access to information". One 7-year-old compared agents on a topic she knew well (sloths).

- The agent should tell children *why* it cannot answer.
- It should distinguish "not understanding the question" from "not having access to a specific information".
- It should scaffold the amount of information it gives.

- A small, preliminary 4-page work-in-progress paper.

**Relevance to DeltaOps**
- Children spontaneously test an AI against things they already know. Labs can invite this: "Ask it about something you know."
- Expressive embodiment, such as eyes and movement, drives attributions of feelings. This supports a toned-down helper character.

## 2. Children, confidence displays and trust calibration

### 2.1 Developmental evidence on confidence and calibration

**Tenney, Small, Kondrad, Jaswal & Spellman (2011)** [ABS] https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023273
- Ages 5–6 and adults.
- Both groups used confidence and accuracy, "however, only adults used information about informants' calibration".
- When adults did a second task at the same time, they also stopped using calibration.

**Birch, Severson & Baimel (2020)** [ABS — new] https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0227026
- Three experiments, N = 662, ages 3–12.
- "Children use a person's history of calibration to guide their learning."
- "Children preferred a well-calibrated over a miscalibrated confident person by around 4 years, whereas even 7- to 8-year-olds were insensitive to calibration in hesitant people."
- **What this means:**
  - Children *can* use calibration when the confident source is shown to be wrong.
  - They are weak at noticing when a *hesitant* source is reliable.
  - For DeltaOps: children may learn "92% sure but wrong, so check". They are less likely to learn "52% sure but right, so low confidence is not the same as wrong" without help.

**Fobert, Varin, Cossette, Fournier & Brosseau-Liard (2024)**, "Children presume confident informants will be accurate (until proven otherwise)" [AGT] https://doi.org/10.1002/icd.2551
- Ages 3–8.
- When confidence and demonstrated accuracy conflicted, older children chose the confident-but-inaccurate informant on 23% of trials where the correct item was at stake (agent summary).

**Baer, Engelmann & Kidd (2025)** [AGT] https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.70027
- N = 92, ages 5–10.
- When two witnesses disagreed with *equal* confidence, children over about 8 years picked a midpoint between them.
- When confidence differed, 8–10-year-olds chose the more confident answer on about 60% of trials.

**Meder, Mayrhofer & Ruggeri (2022)** [AGT] https://doi.org/10.1111/tops.12564
- N = 160 children aged 4–14, plus 44 adults.
- Children placed everyday uncertainty words (e.g. "probably", "sometimes") on an unlabelled slider.
- From age 9, child and adult mean estimates correlated above r = .9.
- Mapping "half" to the midpoint was reliable only from age 8.

**Birch, Akmal & Frampton (2010)** [ABS, unchanged] https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2009.00906.x
- 2–3-year-olds prefer to learn from confident-looking models.

**Danovitch & Alzahabi (2013)** [ABS, re-checked] https://doi.org/10.1080/15248372.2012.689391
- N = 92, ages 3–5.
- Children relied on the "previously accurate computer".
- 4–5-year-olds explained errors as "problems with the device, rather than errors on the part of the human user".

**Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch (2022)** [ABS, re-checked] https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001318
- Ages 4–5 and 7–8.
- "With increasing age, children showed greater trust in the voice assistant for factual information."
- Endorsing a statement predicted later recall.

### 2.2 Children aged 7–13 meeting *numeric* AI confidence (closest evidence; no controlled study)

**Dwivedi, Gandhi, Parikh, Coenraad, Bonsignore & Kacorri (2021)**, *Exploring Machine Teaching with Children* [FT — new] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8783664/
- **Design:** 14 children aged 7–13, two co-design sessions. Children trained Google Teachable Machine, which shows a percentage for each class.
- **Findings:**
  - "Children interpreted confidence scores in three ways":
    - correct class at a stable 100% = success
    - rapid switching between classes = "confused"
    - "if the classifier remained 100% on a wrong class, they viewed it as a mistake or that something had gone wrong".
  - Several children "trained their inputs until they registered a confidence level of 100%". In other words, they treated the score as a target.
  - The authors recommend that "ML metrics (e.g. confidence scores) should be visible for experimentation".
- **Caveats:**
  - These children were experienced co-designers.
  - The study is qualitative and the authors call it "not conclusive".
  - Crucially, the children *knew the right answer* (they held the object). That is exactly the situation in DeltaOps, where the world outcome reveals whether the helper was right.

**Dangol, Wolfe, Landesman, Yip & Kientz (2025)**, AI-Smartlock [AGT] https://adango26.github.io/assets/pdf/25_isls.pdf
- 7 children aged 7–12, one 1.5-hour session.
- Children read class percentages (e.g. "56% shrimp / 44% cat") and adjusted a percentage threshold that opened a simulated door.
- No measure of trust or comprehension was reported.

**Sanusi et al. (2024)** [AGT] https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/08993408.2023.2175559
- 18 Nigerian children aged 3–13 using Teachable Machine in informal settings.
- Children inferred how input relates to output. No measure of calibration.

**Ozturk et al. (2026)**, *How Children Design and Reason about Trustworthy AI Chatbots* [AGT] https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25244
- Elementary and middle-school students set their chatbots to *higher* confidence than high-school students did (adjusted p < .05).
- High-school students set higher transparency.
- Some middle-schoolers called deliberately wrong chatbots trustworthy if the chatbot "did what they were designed to do".

### 2.3 Adult evidence on confidence displays (re-checked)

**Li et al.**, *Understanding the Effects of Miscalibrated AI Confidence…* [ABS] https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07632
- "AI miscalibration is difficult for users to detect."
- Telling users how calibrated the AI is helps them detect miscalibration, but "leading to high under-reliance, it does not improve the decision efficacy".

**Fregosi, Vicente, Campagner & Cabitza (2026)**, *Too Sure for Our Own Good* [ABS — upgraded from SEC] https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/38798
- N = 184 adults, within-subjects design.
- Well-calibrated confidence improved accuracy by **+20%** [0.18, 0.23]. Miscalibrated confidence gave +2%.
- "Participants were more likely to accept AI recommendations when high confidence was expressed, even when those recommendations were incorrect."
- Low-confidence recommendations increased "conservatism bias", leading users "to reject even accurate AI suggestions".

### 2.4 Synthesis and design spec for the "% sure" meter

**Status of the evidence**
- No controlled study shows how 7–13-year-olds interpret a numeric AI confidence display. The gap remains, and DeltaOps could fill it (§9).
- The closest evidence suggests three things:
  - Children readily treat a percentage as a success signal (Dwivedi 2021).
  - They can flag "100% but wrong" when they can see the truth (Dwivedi 2021).
  - They can learn from a *confident* source's track record (Birch 2020), but they discount hesitant-but-right sources (Birch 2020; Fregosi 2026 in adults).

**Spec**

1. **Always let the world reveal the truth.** The lab already does this when the ducks leave. This recreates the one condition under which children in Dwivedi 2021 read "100% on the wrong class" as an error.
2. **Show a running track record**, not a single number. For example, a row of stamps per stage: "Sure and right", "Sure and wrong", "Unsure and right". Birch 2020 shows children use calibration *history*.
3. **Script at least one "unsure but right" stage per lab.** In it, the facilitator says: "It was only 55% sure, and it was right. Not sure doesn't mean wrong." This targets the documented blind spot, where 7–8-year-olds ignore calibration in hesitant sources (Birch 2020), and it guards against under-reliance (Li et al.; Fregosi 2026).
4. **Pair the number with words for ages 7–8.** For example: "really sure (92%)", "not very sure (55%)". Children map uncertainty words onto a scale in an adult-like way from about age 9 (Meder 2022, [AGT]). Words help younger children. Meder found reliable mapping of "half" only from age 8, so avoid relying on a bare 50%.
5. **Never make 100% a target or reward.** Children in Dwivedi 2021 "trained … until … 100%". No stars for high helper confidence.
6. **Use cautious helper diction** ("maybe", "I'm guessing"), as Van Brummelen et al. 2023 suggest.
7. **State the rule aloud every stage:** "Sure isn't the same as right. We check." Tenney 2011 found calibration is cognitively demanding even for adults.

## 3A. Single-session vs multi-session: what the evidence now says

| Source | Finding | Verification |
|---|---|---|
| Clark et al. 2016 | Single session g = 0.08 (ns) vs multiple 0.44 | [FT] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4748544/ |
| Wouters et al. 2013 | One session d = 0.10 (ns) vs multiple 0.54 | [FT] https://ocw.metu.edu.tr/mod/resource/view.php?id=5403 |
| Tokac, Novak & Thompson 2019 (maths games, PreK–12) | Up to 1 hour of play g = −0.03; 1–8 hours 0.19; over 8 hours 0.12. Grades 1–6 g = 0.13 | [SEC, figures from table in] https://repository.usfca.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1671&context=diss ; abstract https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.12347 |
| Wang et al. 2022 (as tabulated by Conmy) | Interventions **under one week** g = 0.95, larger than longer ones | [SEC] same dissertation |
| Behnamnia et al. 2024 (primary STEM games, 18 studies) | Under one week ES = 0.773 (k = 10); 3 months or more ES = 0.18 (k = 1). The authors suggest "reduced novelty effects" for short interventions | [FT table] https://doi.org/10.24310/ijtei.102.2024.19694 |
| Sinha & Kapur 2021 | A few hours g = 0.32 vs a few days 0.41 (ns) | [FT] §1.4 |
| Crystal Bridges RCT (Greene, Kisida & Bowen) | A **single ~1-hour facilitated tour** (5 paintings, discussion). Measured about 3 weeks later (up to 8 weeks): critical thinking about a new painting +0.09 SD (blind-scored essays, 3,811 students); rural students "nearly one-third of a standard deviation"; high-poverty and minority students +0.18 SD. 70–88% recalled painting details. "No indication that the results … faded" | [FT article] https://www.educationnext.org/the-educational-value-of-field-trips/ ; [ABS] https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X13512675 |
| Gutwill & Allen 2010 | Families randomly taught a short inquiry game during **one visit** improved inquiry at a new exhibit: d = 0.3–0.7 | [FT article page] https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sce.20387 |
| Habgood & Ainsworth 2011 | Intrinsic advantage appeared only after multiple sessions plus a delay | [FT] §1.6 |

**How to read the conflict**
- "Under one week" in the duration meta-analyses is not the same as "one session". Those short-duration findings are open to the novelty explanation the authors themselves offer.
- The two meta-analyses that coded *sessions* (Clark; Wouters), plus Tokac's "up to 1 hour" band, all find roughly zero average effect for one-off game play.
- Yet the informal-learning trials (Crystal Bridges; Gutwill & Allen) show that a single, **facilitated** session can produce small but real, measurable effects. This holds especially on narrow, near-transfer outcomes such as observing details or asking a juicy question, and for children with less prior access.

**Realistic target for DeltaOps**
- Aim for a small effect on a narrow, near-transfer skill: noticing an ambiguous instruction, and checking rather than trusting high confidence.
- Plan for d ≈ 0.1–0.3. This is my inference from the figures above, not a measured DeltaOps result.
- Get the rest from repeats: club and camp packages, and take-home prompts.

## 5. Informal learning: museums, facilitation, camps (all [MEM] items now verified)

- **Jant et al. 2014** [FT]: see §1.7.
- **Allen (2004)**, *Designs for learning* [ABS] https://doi.org/10.1002/sce.20016 : immediate apprehendability, physical interactivity, conceptual coherence, diversity of learners.
- **Gutwill & Allen (2010)** [FT article page — verified] https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/sce.20387 ; ERIC https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ896320
  - **Design:** a randomised trial with **200 families** (50 per condition):
    - Juicy Question game
    - Hands-Off game
    - an educator-led "Exhibit Tour" control
    - a no-mediation control.
  - **Skills taught:** "Proposing Actions" and "Interpreting Results".
  - **A "juicy" question** is "a question that can be answered at the exhibit and to which nobody knows the answer".
  - **Results:**
    - Inquiry games beat the tour control (d = 0.3–0.6).
    - Juicy Question beat Hands-Off (d = 0.5–0.7).
    - **The tour control "did not have any effect"** on later inquiry. Educator talk alone did not teach inquiry.
  - The collaborative game won because it "required all family members to participate, work together, and explicitly articulate their interpretations". Hands-Off was "especially popular with younger children, since it gave them a way to be heard over their potentially dominating siblings".
  - About a quarter of families found Juicy Question "rigid". At 3 weeks, roughly a fifth to a third reported using it again.
- **Borun et al., PISEC (1997–98)** [FT of PISEC summary — verified] https://sites.nationalacademies.org/cs/groups/dbassesite/documents/webpage/dbasse_088521.pdf ; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.2151-6952.1997.tb01302.x
  - The seven characteristics of family-friendly exhibits:
    - **multi-sided:** the family can cluster round it
    - **multi-user:** several sets of hands
    - **accessible:** usable by children and adults
    - **multi-outcome:** complex enough "to foster group discussion"
    - **multi-modal:** suits different learning styles and levels of knowledge
    - **readable:** text in easy chunks
    - **relevant:** links to visitors' own knowledge and experience.
  - Exhibits enhanced with these features "measurably increased" family learning: https://www.aam-us.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/EXH_spg08_Why-Family-Learning-in-Museums__Borun.pdf
- **Pattison & Dierking (2012)** [ABS — verified] https://doi.org/10.1179/jme.2012.37.3.69
  - A qualitative study of 63 staff–family interactions.
  - Adult family members "acted as gatekeepers to deeper staff engagement".
- **Pattison et al. (2018), REVEAL** [FT pre-print — verified] https://www.terc.edu/reveal/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2021/03/PattisonEtAl2018_VS_StaffImpact_PrePub.pdf ; summary https://www.terc.edu/reveal/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2021/03/FindingsRecommendationsSummary_REVEAL.pdf
  - **Design:** quasi-experimental, 263 visitor groups, three math exhibits, four trained educators.
  - **Facilitation vs greeting only:**
    - engagement time +3.94 minutes (β = 3.94, p < .001)
    - satisfaction higher
    - mathematical reasoning higher at two of three exhibits
    - **intergenerational communication fell**
    - no effect on math awareness.
  - Differences between exhibits and between educators "were often as large or larger than the effects of the facilitation condition".
  - The REVEAL model is "observe, support, reflect".
- **Serrell (1997; 1998)** [ABS + author's own summaries — verified] https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2151-6952.1997.tb01292.x ; https://www.aam-us.org/2017/11/01/some-thoughts-on-visitor-exhibition-patterns/
  - The study covered 108–110 exhibitions.
  - "Visitors typically spend less than 20 minutes in exhibitions, regardless of the topic or size."

  - No controlled learning-outcome study of a holiday or summer camp AI activity for ages 7–13 was found.
  - Closest examples:

## 9. Recommended evaluation design (revised)

### 9.1 Design
1. **Pre/post within the session.** Three items, parallel forms (A/B), tapped on screen; read aloud for ages 7–8.
2. **Comparison (value-added).** Randomise *sessions* at clubs and camps. Two candidate comparisons:
   - (a) full lab vs the same lab without the facilitator "why" step
   - (b) the track-record confidence meter vs a single-number meter.
   Comparison (b) would be among the first controlled data on children reading numeric AI confidence (§2).
3. **Delayed check at 2–4 weeks** for clubs and schools. Habgood & Ainsworth's group differences appeared only at the delayed test; Crystal Bridges measured at about 3 weeks.
4. **Observation rubric** (§9.3). Two coders on 20% of sessions. Jant et al. reached κ = .96; Crystal Bridges reached α = .94.
5. **Engagement log**: time per stage, choices, retries. Never treat it as a learning measure.
6. **Ethics**: no personal data; session codes; consent for observation.

### 9.2 The 3-item exit check
1. **Assumptions (transfer).** "A robot gardener is told 'Make the garden tidy.' What might it do that you DIDN'T want?" Choose 1 of 3 pictures. Optional follow-up: which instruction is better?
2. **Confidence ≠ correctness (Tenney/Birch paradigm, revised).** "Helper A said 'really sure, 95%' and was wrong twice. Helper B said 'not very sure, 55%' and was right both times. Which helper should we check more carefully next time?" Answers: A / B / both the same.
   - Optional item for the hesitant-source blind spot (Birch 2020): "Helper B says 'not very sure' again. Should we ignore it?" Answers: Yes / No, check / It's always wrong.
3. **Agency (Druga & Ko).** "Why did the helper get it wrong?" Options:
   - (a) it was being naughty
   - (b) it guessed from what it had learned, and the instruction was unclear
   - (c) it's broken.
   Score anthropomorphic answers separately.

**Sample size** (my calculation, not a sourced figure)
- To detect a within-child pre/post change of d = 0.3 with 80% power at α = .05 (two-sided, paired), you need about 90 children with both forms.
- For d = 0.2, about 200.
- A session-level comparison needs considerably more because of clustering.
- Pilot with about 60 children first, to check item difficulty and discrimination.

### 9.3 Observation rubric (per group, per stage, scored 0/1/2)

| Code | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prediction | None before reveal | Prediction made | Prediction with a reason |
| Explains AI behaviour | "It's silly" | Describes what happened | Mechanistic: "it matched 'nice' to 'tidy'" |
| Anthropomorphic talk (count) | wants / feels / naughty | — | — |
| References confidence | Ignores the meter | Notes the % | "It was sure but wrong" / "unsure but right" |
| Revises instruction | Random pick | Picks the clearer card | Says *what* made it clearer |
| Peer and family talk (new; REVEAL) | Only facilitator–child talk | Child–child or child–adult exchange | Disagreement resolved with evidence |
| Tests against own knowledge (new; Druga 2017) | — | Child checks the helper against something they know | Proposes a test |
| Facilitator move | Tells the answer | Asks a question | Asks, waits, revoices the child's idea, then hands back to the group |

### 9.4 Analysis and reporting
- Use McNemar tests or mixed models, clustered by session.
- Report effect sizes with confidence intervals by age band (7–9 vs 10–13), and by prior access where you can.
- Crystal Bridges found effects 2–3 times larger for rural and high-poverty pupils.
- Publish the results. §3 shows no controlled outcome data exist for informal AI-literacy activities at this age.

## 10. Facilitator script template (per stage, revised)

Grounded in:
- Wouters 2013 (other instruction alongside the game; group play)
- Alfieri 2011 (elicited explanation)
- Sinha & Kapur 2021 (explicit goal; quick "why" for younger pupils; few strong moves)
- Jant 2014 (wh-questions)
- Birch 2020 and Tenney 2011 (calibration made explicit; include unsure-but-right)
- Gutwill & Allen 2010 (a questioning routine everyone voices)
- Pattison et al. 2018 (observe, support, reflect; hand talk back to the family)
- Druga et al. 2017 (test it against what you know)

```
STAGE [n] — [Island]: [Goal, e.g. "Make the pond good for ducks AND visitors"]
Time budget: 4–6 min

1. SET THE GOAL (20 s)            [explicit goal — Sinha & Kapur]
   Say: "Our goal is ___. The helper only knows the words we give it."

2. PREDICT TOGETHER (40 s)        [elicited prediction; everyone voices]
   Read the three cards. "What will the helper do with THIS one?"
   Go round: every child (and adult) says one guess. Don't correct yet.

3. CHOOSE & REVEAL (30 s)
   Read the guess and its sureness in words AND number:
   "It's REALLY sure — 92%." Ask: "Do we think it's right?"

4. NOTICE (40 s)                  [wh-question; world shows the truth]
   "What happened to the [ducks/reeds]? Why do you think it did that?"
   Model answer if stuck: "It matched 'nice' to 'tidy'. Tidy has no reeds."

5. EXPLAIN IT PLAINLY (20 s)      [explicit telling; essential for 7–10]
   "It didn't know what WE meant. It guessed from the word 'nice'.
   Clear words → better guesses." Use process words, never feelings.

6. CONFIDENCE CHECK (30 s)        [calibration made explicit]
   Point to the track-record stamps: "It was really sure — was it right?
   Sure isn't the same as right. We check."
   On the scripted UNSURE-BUT-RIGHT stage: "Only 55% sure — and right!
   Not sure doesn't mean wrong. We still check."

7. RETRY (60 s)                   [guided discovery]
   "Which card says exactly what we want? Which words make it clearer?"
   One child explains the choice to a partner or grown-up before tapping.

8. COMPARE (20 s)                 [contrasting cases]
   Before/after side by side: "What's different in the words? In the world?"

Facilitator DON'Ts: don't give the answer before step 5; don't say the AI
"thinks/feels/wants"; don't let one child drive — rotate the tapper;
don't become the only person talking — after you ask, step back and let
families and pairs talk to each other (REVEAL found facilitators can
crowd out family talk).
```

**Closing debrief** (3 minutes, whole group)
1. "What's one thing the helper did that surprised you?"
2. "Finish the sentence: AI helpers follow instructions ___." (literally)
3. "When it says it's very sure, what do we do?" (check) "And when it's not sure?" (still check; it might be right)
4. "Next time you ask a computer or voice assistant something, how could you test it?" (ask it something you already know; Druga 2017)
5. Take-home family card (Jant 2014; LM design consideration 10), printed on the artefact. It carries one "juicy question" in Gutwill & Allen's sense, for example: "Ask a voice assistant something you know the answer to. Was it right? How sure did it sound?"

## 11. Updated implications for DeltaOps (supersede round 2)

1. **Plan for small, narrow, measurable effects in one session, and sell repeats.**
   - One-off game play averages about zero (Clark; Wouters; Tokac).
   - Single *facilitated* visits do produce small real effects (Crystal Bridges +0.09 SD; Gutwill & Allen d = 0.3–0.7).
2. **The debrief is the lesson, and it is now controlled evidence, not only consensus.** In Habgood & Ainsworth, the control group, which played a game with no maths, improved significantly after the teacher's reflection lesson.
3. **Make the goal explicit, scaffold the failure, then explain quickly** for ages 7–10. Grades 2–5 g = −0.09 and domain-general skills g = −0.17 (Sinha & Kapur [FT]).
4. **Don't cram.** In short interventions, more design features meant lower effects (Sinha & Kapur). Keep 6 stages, each with one point.
5. **Redesign the confidence meter** (§2.4): track record; an unsure-but-right stage; words plus numbers for 7–8s; no reward for 100%. Children's blind spot is the hesitant-but-right source (Birch 2020).
6. **Use the world as ground truth.** Children read "100% and wrong" as an error only when they can see the truth (Dwivedi 2021).
7. **Process language, not character language.** Expressive eyes and movement made children credit Cozmo with feelings (Druga 2017). Experience AI's independent evaluation saw no significant drop in anthropomorphism without explicit teaching (Waite et al.). So *teach* it explicitly ("Does it know, or is it matching?"), not only by avoiding human-like wording.
8. **Give families a routine, not a lecture.**
   - Educator tour talk had no effect on later inquiry (Gutwill & Allen).
   - Facilitation can lower family talk (REVEAL).
   - So script questions that everyone answers, and hand talk back to the group.
9. **Invite children to test the helper against what they know.** Children do this spontaneously (Druga 2017). AILit's *Shape AI* names "test cases".
10. **Cooperative, multi-user play** (Wouters groups d = 0.66; PISEC; Clark competitive −0.06).
11. **Keep visuals schematic and the story thin but emotionally engaging** (Clark; Wouters; Sinha & Kapur on affective draw).
12. **Map to the 2026 frameworks in sales materials:**
    - AILit (final, 19 competences, explicitly endorses offline and low-tech activities)
    - England's commitment that pupils learn "how computers can be trained using data"
    - KS2 debugging (statutory until 2028).
    Don't cite draft programmes-of-study wording until it is published.
13. **Run the §9 evaluation and publish it.** No controlled outcome data exist for informal AI-literacy activities at this age (§3). Interland shows knowledge can rise without behaviour change, so don't over-claim.
