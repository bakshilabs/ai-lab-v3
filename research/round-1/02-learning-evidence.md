## Evidence brief: world-building and AI-literacy games for children (ages 7–13)

**About the sources.** Most sources below were checked through search abstracts and summaries, not full texts. The DeltaOps implications are my own inferences from the evidence. None of them has been tested on DeltaOps itself.

### 1. Educational world-builders: what has evidence
- **Minecraft (incl. Education Edition):** A 2025 systematic review found 29 studies. It reports positive effects on spatial thinking, creativity, maths, science and language, but **every study had medium or high risk of bias** ([Slattery et al. 2025](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/assessing-the-benefits-of-digital-gamebased-learning-with-minecraft-in-children-adolescents-and-young-adults-a-broad-systematic-review/)). An earlier review was more favourable on motivation and on science/history learning ([Alawajee & Delafield-Butt 2021](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/78811)). In short: promising, not proven.
- **SimCityEDU / GlassLab:** Its main contribution was research into in-game assessment (ETS/SRI). I could not find a published learning-outcome result ([EdSurge review](https://www.edsurge.com/n/2013-11-24-review-going-to-town-on-simcityedu); [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/32389)).
- **Eco (Strange Loop Games):** Development was funded by IES (US Institute of Education Sciences) grants. In a pilot with 88 students and a comparison group, players scored higher on environmental attitudes and systems knowledge. Students' chat showed STEM and civics talk during shared play ([IES SBIR](https://ies.ed.gov/sbir/ECO.asp); [InformalScience](https://informalscience.org/project/eco-online-virtual-world-secondary-school-environmental-literacy-and-collaborative-problem-solving/)).
- **Civilization:** The classic evidence is qualitative. In Squire's (2004) dissertation, history learning came from teacher-led discussion around play, not from play alone.
- **Kerbal Space Program EDU, Roblox education, zoo/park tycoons:** I found no rigorous outcome studies. The evidence is anecdotal.
- **Foldit:** Players beat an automated algorithm on hard protein-refinement problems, and the paper was published in *Nature*. The "you are doing real work" framing drove engagement, but Foldit is evidence of player contribution, not of what players learned ([Cooper et al. 2010](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/cooper2010pps.pdf)).

### 2. AI literacy for children
- **Frameworks:** Long & Magerko define 17 competencies (e.g. recognising AI, understanding its strengths and weaknesses, the role of data, and that "humans make AI"). They also give design considerations, including explainability, embodiment, contextualisation, and attention to misconceptions ([CHI 2020](https://aiunplugged.lmc.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2020/08/CHI-2020-AI-Literacy-Paper-Camera-Ready.pdf)). AI4K12 organises the field into five big ideas: Perception, Representation & Reasoning, Learning, Natural Interaction, and Societal Impact ([AI4K12](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/ai-education-ai4k12-big-ideas-ai-thinking/)).
- **Children's mental models:** Children aged 3–10 judged smart agents friendly and trustworthy, and younger children struggled more ([Druga & Breazeal 2017](https://robots.media.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2017/06/idcwp0180-drugaACR.pdf)). In PopBots, young children learned some AI concepts (about 67% mean on assessments), and their views of robots as "smart" or "people-like" differed by age ([Williams et al. 2019](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5040)).
  - Children rated agents as more human-like and dependable than parents did, and trusted them more than parents or friends for correct information.
  - Trust shifted most when children learned about an agent's information sources. The authors recommend making competence, predictability and sources visible ([Van Brummelen et al., AAAI 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2209.05063)).
- **Teachable Machine and similar data-driven tools:** Qualitative studies show children grasping training data, classification, *confidence* and brittleness by building and testing their own models ([Vartiainen et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.18688)).
- **Code.org AI for Oceans, Quick, Draw!, Machine Learning for Kids:** These are very widely used. I found no published outcome evaluations.
- **Day of AI (MIT RAISE):** Its evaluation was commissioned by the programme and relies on self-report. It reports gains in knowledge and optimism ([Day of AI 2023](https://dayofai.org/?p=859)).
- **Interland (Google's Be Internet Awesome):** A cluster RCT with 1,072 children found gains in knowledge and self-efficacy. Effects on behaviour are unclear ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40688-023-00465-5)).
- **Museum exhibits:** Long and Magerko's group built three exhibits (Knowledge Net, Creature Features, LuminAI). Their design principles are: centre learner interests, produce personally meaningful outputs, use embodiment, and support collaboration ([InformalScience](https://informalscience.org/research/fostering-ai-literacy-with-embodiment-creativity-from-activity-boxes-to-museum-exhibits/)).

### 3. Learning-design evidence
- **Games work modestly, and design matters more than the medium.** Games beat non-game instruction (g = 0.33). Enhanced designs beat basic ones by a similar margin (0.34). Simpler visuals were as good or better ([Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth 2016](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/YHX5ZVR8)).
- Serious games gave d = 0.29 for learning but were *not* reliably more motivating. They worked better when combined with other instruction, run over multiple sessions, and played **in groups** ([Wouters et al. 2013](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-cognitive-and-motivational-effects-of-seri/)).
- **Instructional support** (reflection, modelling, feedback, collaboration) added d = 0.34 ([Wouters & van Oostendorp 2013](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13608021/05.pdf)).
- **Debrief:** Crookall argues that learning from games happens largely *in the debriefing*, and that skipping it is the most common failure. This is a strong practitioner consensus, though the controlled evidence is thinner ([Crookall](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/1046878110390784)).
- **Guided, not pure, discovery:** Unassisted discovery underperformed explicit instruction (d = −0.38). Enhanced discovery, with feedback, scaffolds and elicited explanations, outperformed other instruction (d = 0.30) ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf)).
- **Productive failure:** Problem-solving first, then instruction, gave g = 0.87 after adjusting for publication bias. However, the advantage **reversed for grades 2–5** ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704)).
- **Contrasting cases:** Comparing cases side by side helps learners notice key features, which prepares them for the explanation that follows ([Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf)).
- **Constructionism (Papert, *Mindstorms*):** Learners understand best by building things and debugging them.
- **Short informal sessions:** Giving parents conversation cards with elaborative "wh-" questions increased explanatory talk at a museum exhibit. That kind of talk predicts later remembering and transfer ([Jant et al. 2014](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/uttal/vittae/documents/Jant_et_al-2014-Child_Development.pdf)).

### 4. Pitfalls
- **Chocolate-covered broccoli:** Zombie Division (ages 7–11) was built so the learning mechanic *is* the core game mechanic ("intrinsic integration"). That version produced more learning than a version where learning was bolted on, and children played it about 7x longer by choice ([Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/)).
- **Extraneous load and visual complexity:** Simpler visuals were as effective or better ([Clark et al. 2016](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/YHX5ZVR8)).
- **Novelty effects:** Effects are larger in short interventions and fade after a few weeks ([Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)). One-off labs should measure learning, not enjoyment.
- **Confidence read as correctness:** Adults accept AI advice that comes with high stated confidence even when it is wrong. Only *well-calibrated* confidence improved their decisions ([AAAI 2026](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/38798); [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07632v3)). Children already tend to over-trust agents (Van Brummelen above).
- **Anthropomorphism:** Young children struggle to tell simulated abilities from real ones ([arXiv 2512.02179](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02179v1)). A friendly, named "helper" with feelings may strengthen this.

## Implications for DeltaOps labs

1. **Treat the facilitator debrief as the lesson, not an add-on.** Give each stage a short script: what happened, why, what next. Close with a 2-minute whole-lab wrap-up that names the four lessons explicitly. ([Crookall](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/reader/10.1177/1046878110390784); [Wouters & van Oostendorp 2013](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13608021/05.pdf))
2. **Keep the vague-card-first sequence, but always follow the failure with explicit explanation.** For 7–10-year-olds, put a short "telling" moment (the helper's literal interpretation, made visible) straight after the failure, not at the end. Productive failure reversed for grades 2–5. ([Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704); [Alfieri 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf))
3. **Show the vague and clear outcomes side by side.** A split-screen of "Make it nice" (reeds cut, ducks gone) next to "Tidy the path but keep the reeds" (ducks stay) lets children spot what made the difference. ([Schwartz & Bransford 1998](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf))
4. **Fix the confidence meter (highest risk).** If "% sure" is high when the AI is wrong and the lesson stops there, children may learn either "high % means right" or "the % means nothing."
   - Script cases where confidence was well-founded *and* cases where it was not.
   - Add a "check the evidence" step: a magnifier that shows *why* the helper guessed, e.g. "'nice' usually means tidy in my examples."
   - Have the facilitator ask, "It was 92% sure. Was it right? How could we check?"
   - ([AAAI 2026](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/38798); [Van Brummelen 2023](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2209.05063))
5. **Show where the helper's guesses come from.** One line of "what I learned from" per guess targets the trust lever the research found most effective (information sources) and covers AI4K12's "computers learn from data" idea. ([Van Brummelen](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2209.05063); [AI4K12](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/ai-education-ai4k12-big-ideas-ai-thinking/))
6. **Tone down the helper's personality.** Avoid feelings, a human name and phrases like "I want"; call it a tool, e.g. "the guesser." Add a facilitator prompt: "Does it *know* what nice means, or is it matching patterns?" ([Druga & Breazeal 2017](https://robots.media.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2017/06/idcwp0180-drugaACR.pdf); [Long & Magerko 2020](https://aiunplugged.lmc.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2020/08/CHI-2020-AI-Literacy-Paper-Camera-Ready.pdf))
7. **Keep the lesson inside the core mechanic.** Choosing an instruction and seeing the world change already does this. Resist adding points, badges or mini-games that sit beside the learning. ([Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/))
8. **Build for groups.** Have pairs or small teams agree on a card before choosing, and add a "predict what the AI will do" vote before each reveal. Group play and elicited predictions both improve learning. ([Wouters et al. 2013](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-cognitive-and-motivational-effects-of-seri/); [Alfieri 2011](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf))
9. **Add one constructionist step at the end.** In stage 6, children assemble their *own* instruction from fragments (verb + object + "but keep…"), then see it run. Choosing from 3 cards alone is recognition, not production. ([Papert/constructionism](https://informalscience.org/research/fostering-ai-literacy-with-embodiment-creativity-from-activity-boxes-to-museum-exhibits/))
10. **Write facilitator cards with elaborative "why/how" questions and model answers.** Staff vary in skill, and these cards measurably raise explanatory talk. Offer a take-home card for parents. ([Jant et al. 2014](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/uttal/vittae/documents/Jant_et_al-2014-Child_Development.pdf))
11. **Keep the voxel world visually simple** so attention stays on cause and effect (instruction → AI guess → ecological consequence). ([Clark et al. 2016](https://docs.opendeved.net/lib/YHX5ZVR8))
12. **Evaluate learning, not enjoyment.** Use a 3-item exit check, e.g. a new vague instruction with "What might the AI assume?" and "It says 95% sure, should we trust it?". Try a delayed check where possible. Most AI-literacy activities (AI for Oceans, Day of AI) lack outcome data, and novelty inflates satisfaction in one-off sessions. Measured outcomes would set DeltaOps apart. ([Rodrigues et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6); [Day of AI eval](https://dayofai.org/?p=859))
13. **Teach "sometimes right, sometimes wrong — check," not "AI is always wrong."** Include at least one stage where a clear instruction gives a correct, well-founded high-confidence result. ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07632v3) on disuse of under-trusted AI)
